# Writing style

Derived from `src/content/blog/hello-universe.md` (2026-10-05), the only published post. Treat
it as the house voice until more posts exist, then re-check this file against the newer ones.

## Voice

- First person, reflective, unhurried. Daniel is thinking out loud with the reader, not
  announcing conclusions: "I suspect", "I've found myself", "my own rule has become".
- Argues against a false binary and settles somewhere in the middle. The post rejects
  "is agentic development good or bad" as the wrong question before proposing a better one.
- Inclusive "we/our/us" for the industry ("many of us let go of the wheel"), "I" for his own
  practice. Never second-person marketing ("you'll love this").
- Confident where he has a rule, hedged where he is guessing. Hedge words are load-bearing:
  "probably", "in practice", "maybe". Do not stack hedges into mush.
- Dry, understated humour instead of jokes: "Other times, it confidently drives directly into
  a wall." Deadpan, one clause, never signposted.

## Shape

- Opens on a familiar idea or cliché the reader already believes, then turns it: "It's been a
  polarising time...", "If you want something done right, do it yourself is a cliché for a
  reason."
- One extended everyday metaphor per essay, carried to the last line. Here it is driving: the
  wheel, driving into a wall, a self-driving car, "my hands close enough to the wheel".
  Pick the metaphor early and return to it in the close.
- Section headings are lowercase, short and conversational: "taking the wheel", "following
  your guidance", "be careful what you ask for", "the weird middle". Headings are signposts,
  not summaries, and numbering is used only when it helps (1., 2., then dropped).
- Ends on a personal, resolved beat that calls back the opening image. No summary, no
  "in conclusion", no call to action, no links, no lists.

## Rhythm

- Paragraphs run two to five sentences. A single-sentence paragraph is the punch:
  "At some point, many of us let go of the wheel."
- Long, comma-rolling sentences alternate with short declaratives. The short one carries the
  point; the long one sets it up.
- Colons introduce elaboration, lists of questions, or the payoff. Semicolons are rare (one in
  the whole post) and em dashes are absent.
- Lists of specifics beat abstractions: "add a field, change an API, fix a bug, write tests,
  refactor something".

## Rhetorical moves (reuse these, in moderation)

- **The rule in bold**: one sentence, stated plainly, then defended.
  "**if you don't feel comfortable with what you're producing using agents, don't ship it.**"
- **Question clusters** to expose vagueness: three or four questions in a row, ending on the
  one that matters.
- **Concession, then reversal**: "That sounds trivial, but...", "That's impressive in the same
  way a self-driving car completing a journey is impressive, but it isn't necessarily the
  metric that matters."
- **Distinction as thesis**: "handing over execution and handing over judgement are two very
  different things."
- **Scepticism of metrics and hype**: measure the result, not how little you did.
- **Analogy to non-AI engineering practice**: tech leads and architects have always done this.

## Diction and mechanics

- British spelling throughout: learnt, polarising, optimise/optimising, behaviour.
- Curly apostrophes and quotes. Double quotes for quoted speech and hypotheticals, italics for
  a single emphasised word (_good_), bold only for the core rule.
- Intensifiers used sparingly and with intent: genuinely, fairly, surprisingly, ridiculously,
  extraordinarily.
- Concrete nouns and plain verbs: field, test, build, endpoint, wheel. No jargon scaffolding
  ("paradigm", "leverage", "synergy", "unlock").
- No exclamation marks, no emoji, no bullet lists in prose, no code blocks in essay posts.
- Frontmatter: lowercase title ("hello universe"), one long third-person description naming
  the themes, lowercase kebab-case tags, single quotes.

## Avoid

- Listicles, "5 lessons", numbered takeaways, bolded key phrases every other paragraph.
- Openers like "In today's fast-paced world" or "As developers, we all know".
- Hedging every claim, or the opposite: absolutes about the future of the industry.
- Explaining the joke or the metaphor.
- Summary paragraphs that restate the headings.

## Quick check before shipping a post

1. Is there one clear idea, stated as a rule or distinction within the first third?
2. Does one everyday metaphor run from start to finish and land in the last line?
3. Are the headings lowercase, short and signposting rather than summarising?
4. Does any paragraph restate a heading or the intro? Delete it.
5. Read one paragraph aloud: if every sentence is the same length, break one.
